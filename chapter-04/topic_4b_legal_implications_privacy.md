## Intellectual Property Rights and Generative AI

Intellectual property (IP) rights protect creative works, including books, music, artwork, inventions, and even software code, the moment they are created. Given someone's notes and files, these rights are typically straightforward to prove.

Generative AI complicates IP issues because it generates content based on existing data, some of which may be copyrighted. This raises questions about ownership, copyright infringement, and fair use.

### Copyright and AI Training Data

Sometimes, models are knowingly and unknowingly trained on protected material without explicit permission. This can lead to unintentional copyright infringement. For example, if an AI-generated story mimics the style of a famous author, they may have the right to pursue legal action against the prompt creator and/or the AI company. The same applies to unauthorized use of logos, brand names, or slogans in AI-generated content that intentionally or unintentionally violate trademark laws.

The consequences include fines or lawsuits that can reach the millions in extreme cases. As a result, AI developers and users must obtain licenses or permissions before using any protected content. For example, if an AI tool uses a famous song in a video, the copyright holder's permission is required.

To further complicate matters, these laws vary from country to country. Several parts of the world have started to pursue more clarity surrounding AI laws, but little has come of any of these cases thus far. Some legal experts argue that AI-generated content should follow traditional copyright laws as the output is still unique from user to user, while others advocate for new regulations entirely.

Users can protect themselves by using public domain content or works no longer protected by copyright, such as classic literature or historical documents, or finding Creative Commons licenses. This latter agreement means a creator has agreed to share their work and let others reuse it with proper credit and attribution.

## Responsible AI Use

Transparency builds trust and accountability, especially in generative AI, which has become contentious among some. When AI creates content, transparency involves clearly documenting the tools, data sources, and processes throughout the input and output phases.

For example, if a company uses AI to write product descriptions, clearly stating this helps customers understand the origin of the content. This transparency builds credibility and avoids potential claims of misleading advertising.

These disclosures are not legally mandated (at least not currently), but they are the right thing to do, and that is exactly the mindset all professionals should have when they engage with these tools.

## Other Legal Risks

Users can be held liable if their AI-generated content unintentionally spreads false or misleading information about a person or business, as this is known in the legal world as defamation.

Additionally, training AI models can cause more legal troubles if they improperly handle personal information, which can lead to identity theft or other privacy violations. Personal data includes names, addresses, financial details, biometric data like facial recognition and fingerprint scans, or any other piece of information that can be traced back to a specific individual. Behavioral data like browsing habits, purchase history, and online interactions can be hacked and used for nefarious purposes.

Unfortunately, data risks are not all that uncommon. AI models often collect more data than necessary, sometimes without explicit user consent. At the same time, some laws mandate how data can be used and the rights of individuals to know what is being collected.

For example:

- **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR):** These regulations outline data protection and privacy for EU citizens.
- **California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA):** This state law protects consumer data in California.
- **Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA):** This law mandates how healthcare providers can store and use patient data.

## Identity Theft, Synthetic Identities, and Blackmail

In addition to improper training and/or storage, bad actors can use generative AI to create synthetic identities by combining real and fake data to commit financial fraud or other crimes. For example, AI-generated emails or text messages can launch compelling phishing attacks to steal personal information. Using AI for these purposes usually involves deepfakes or AI-generated videos or images that misrepresent individuals. It can also include blackmail or threatening to release private or damaging information about someone unless they pay money or do a particular favor. Generative AI gives criminals the power to create false documents and deepfakes and manipulate personal data for their own gain.

### How Generative AI Can Be Used in Identity Theft

**Example:** A criminal might generate an image of a CEO in a morally compromising position, like committing a robbery. They then reach out directly to the CEO and threaten to release the image to news sources unless the CEO agrees to pay them millions.

## Data Privacy and Security

Data privacy shifts the focus away from who owns the creative work and toward who controls the information behind it. Data privacy questions focus on safety related to what information enters the model, how it might be stored, and what could happen if it falls into the wrong hands.

Many generative models learn from enormous datasets gathered across the open internet. If personal information like names, emails, addresses, medical references, or private correspondence appears in those sources, it can inadvertently become embedded in a training set permanently. Company documents face similar risks. Internal slide decks, contract templates, onboarding materials, or proprietary process notes can surface in online repositories and fall into the hands of bad actors before anyone notices.

Even if a model cannot reproduce these documents verbatim, it may generate fragments or patterns that resemble them. This is why organizations stress caution: once private data enters a model trained on public information, neither the creator nor the company can fully control where that data travels next.

## Case Study: The AI-Generated Marketing Campaign

A startup company wants to launch a new product and uses an AI tool to generate marketing materials, including social media posts, promotional videos, and product descriptions. The team inputs keywords and requests an energetic, humorous tone to appeal to their target demographic of younger adults. After reviewing the output, a team member recognizes a joke they recently heard on a comedy special on TV. Based on historical data, the team believes the new ad will perform well. However, they now worry about potential copyright issues and whether the humor might offend certain audiences.

### Potential Action Steps

1. **Check for Copyright Concerns**
   - Research whether the phrases and jokes are copyrighted or widely used in public domain conversations.
2. **Seek Legal Advice**
   - Consult a legal expert to determine if the content risks copyright infringement or plagiarism claims.
3. **Revise and Customize**
   - Modify the AI-generated content to better align with the brand's unique voice instead of using something already done.
4. **Check Sentiment**
   - Research whether the humor is inclusive and appropriate for all audiences or if it may cause unintentional harm to specific groups or individuals.
5. **Issue a Disclaimer**
   - If the team decides to use parts of the AI-generated content, they can include a disclaimer to maintain transparency with their audience.

Some of the world's largest companies are beginning to test generative AI in their blogs, advertising, and websites, though the public sentiment is still rather mixed. This will likely continue to change as generative AI becomes as commonplace as the Internet, laptops, and smartphones.
